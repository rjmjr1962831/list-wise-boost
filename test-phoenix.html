Discover Phoenix, Arizona

Phoenix rises from the Sonoran Desert as America's fifth-largest city, a sun-drenched metropolis where ancient Hohokam irrigation canals still channel water beneath a skyline of glass towers and terra cotta rooftops. The city pulses with an energy born of constant reinvention, from its aerospace roots to today's semiconductor boom, while never losing its connection to the dramatic desert landscape that defines life here. Morning hikers scramble up Camelback Mountain as the sun paints the McDowell Mountains gold, and by evening those same trails glow beneath a canopy of stars unmarred by coastal humidity.

History of Phoenix

  • 1The Hohokam people engineered over 135 miles of irrigation canals between 300 BC and 1450 AD, creating one of the most sophisticated pre-Columbian water management systems in North America. When Anglo settlers arrived in the 1860s, they cleaned out and expanded these ancient waterways rather than building new ones.
  • 2Jack Swilling, a Confederate veteran and prospector, founded the modern settlement in 1867 after recognizing the agricultural potential of the Salt River Valley. Fellow pioneer Darrell Duppa suggested the name 'Phoenix,' envisioning a new civilization rising from the ruins of the Hohokam.
  • 3During World War II, Phoenix transformed from a small agricultural city into a military and manufacturing hub. Luke Field trained more fighter pilots than any other base in the country, establishing defense and aerospace industries that still anchor the economy today.

Things to Do in Phoenix

Desert Botanical Garden: Spanning 140 acres in Papago Park, this living museum showcases over 50,000 desert plants from arid regions worldwide, with evening flashlight tours revealing the secret nocturnal life of the Sonoran Desert.
Heard Museum: Founded in 1929, this institution houses one of the world's finest collections of American Indian art. The annual Indian Fair & Market draws over 600 artists from more than 100 tribes.
Taliesin West: Frank Lloyd Wright's winter home and architecture school, now a UNESCO World Heritage Site, demonstrates his philosophy of organic architecture with buildings that seem to grow from the desert floor.
South Mountain Park and Preserve: At nearly 17,000 acres with over 51 miles of trails, this is one of the largest municipal parks in North America, offering views from Dobbins Lookout 2,330 feet above the valley.
Musical Instrument Museum: Housing over 8,000 instruments from 200 countries, MIM lets visitors experience global music through wireless headsets that activate as you approach each exhibit.
Roosevelt Row Arts District: Phoenix's creative heart where murals cover entire building facades and First Friday art walks draw thousands to galleries, studios, and restaurants in converted warehouses.

Life in Phoenix

Life in Phoenix revolves around the outdoors, but not in the way coastal cities might expect. The day begins before dawn with hikers conquering Piestewa Peak and cyclists spinning along the Arizona Canal path while temperatures remain bearable. By mid-morning, the city retreats indoors, emerging again as the sun drops and restaurant patios fill with diners watching the sky turn from orange to purple. The cultural calendar peaks from October through April, when the weather draws snowbirds, spring training fans, and festival-goers to events like the Arizona State Fair and Dia de los Muertos celebrations.

Why People Move to Phoenix

  • •Booming Tech Economy: TSMC is building a $65 billion fabrication complex in north Phoenix, the largest foreign investment in U.S. history. Intel operates multiple fabs in Chandler, and the region has become a magnet for data centers, autonomous vehicle testing, and aerospace innovation.
  • •Unmatched Outdoor Access: Within city limits, Phoenix offers more hiking trails than any other major U.S. metro. The Phoenix Mountains Preserve, Papago Park, and South Mountain provide hundreds of miles of trails minutes from downtown.
  • •Year-Round Sunshine: Phoenix averages 299 sunny days per year, more than any other major U.S. city. Winter highs in the 60s and 70s draw golfers to over 200 courses.
  • •Affordable Compared to Coastal Metros: Despite rapid appreciation, Phoenix's median home price of approximately $415,000 remains roughly half that of Los Angeles and a third of San Francisco.

Who's Moving to Phoenix

Phoenix draws buyers from across the economic spectrum, united by a desire for sunshine and value. California refugees represent the largest contingent, trading coastal home equity for twice the square footage. Tech workers cluster in Tempe, Scottsdale, and the Arcadia corridor. Young professionals gravitate toward downtown and Roosevelt Row, while retirees continue their decades-long migration to Sun City, Anthem, and East Valley suburbs. First-time buyers increasingly look to West Valley cities like Buckeye, Goodyear, and Surprise.

Phoenix Real Estate Market Trends

The Phoenix housing market has cooled from its pandemic frenzy but remains fundamentally healthy. After peaking in mid-2022, prices corrected roughly 8-10% before stabilizing. The current median sale price hovers around $415,000 for single-family homes. Inventory has loosened significantly, with active listings up nearly 20% year-over-year. Days on market have stretched to around 60-74 days. Analysts project modest appreciation of 3-5% through 2026, supported by continued population growth and improving affordability.
